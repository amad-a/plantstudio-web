How PlantStudio grows a plant

A PlantStudio plant isn't a drawing. It's about 350 numbers and a random seed. The program grows those numbers into a branching structure one simulated day at a time, then draws the structure with a 3D turtle. Every picture on this page is drawn by the program's own engine (src/model/), running live in your browser, and every code excerpt is read from the source as it is now.

Open PlantStudio itself to edit any of these parameters on the plants.

1. A plant is a sequence of days

A plant has an age at maturity (100 days for most). To show a plant at day 40, the program starts from nothing and runs nextDay() forty times. Nothing is stored between ages: a plant at any age is recomputed from day zero, the same way every time. 


        

          

          

            

            

              
              
              
              
            

            
    
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Each day does four things, in this order:
    
        
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
            A day works out how much the plant as a whole should weigh now (section 3), shares the
            difference out among its parts (section 4), and then lets every part take its own day:
            meristems may build new stem, flowers open, fruit ripens. Weights are in
            % of mature plant biomass (_pctMPB), so a full-grown plant weighs
            close to 100.
          
    
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    2. The seed
    
      
    
        
    
          
    
            Plants vary: a stem curves a little more or less, a branch does or doesn't start, a
            petal sways. All of that comes from one pseudo-random generator, Park and Miller's
            "minimal standard" generator from 1988, started from a seed stored in the plant file.
          
    
        
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
            Every new part draws a number for its sway as it's created, and every branching decision
            draws another. Because the seed is reset at day zero, the same seed always grows the same
            plant. That also means the order of every random call is part of the file format:
            the port had to make exactly the same calls in exactly the same order as the 2002
            program, or the saved plants would grow into different shapes.

            reproducible chance: press the button for the same seed again and you get the same plant.
            Normally distributed values (used for curving and for breeding) are made from twelve of
            these numbers at once:
          
    
        
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
            Adding up twelve uniform numbers between 0 and 1 gives a mean of 6 and a standard
            deviation of exactly 1, and the sum is very close to a bell curve. It's an old trick
            that needs no square roots or logarithms.
          
    
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    3. How much to grow: the S-curve
    
      
    
        
    
          
    
            Real plants grow slowly at first, quickly in the middle, and slowly again near maturity.
            PlantStudio's growth curve says what fraction of its mature weight the plant should have
            reached for each fraction of its life. The curve is set by two points it passes through,
            and the program turns those into two coefficients once, when the plant loads:
          
    
        
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
            
            
          
    
          
    
            
    
            
    
              The curve's two points
              
    
              
    
              
    
              
    
              
            
    
            
    
            
    
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
            Move the points and the plant under the chart is regrown under the new curve. The
            bundled plants' curves level off close to the top by maturity; other points can make a
            plant that never reaches full size, or one that does nearly all its growing in its first
            weeks. The same
            S-curve function also sets how fast each leaf and each fruit fills out, each with its
            own pair of points.
          
    
          
    
            No choice of points gets past 100%: the curve only ever approaches 1, and the program
            stops the clock at maturity anyway. Section 12 takes both
            limits away to see what the rest of the model does with more.
          
    
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    4. Who gets it: biomass allocation
    
      
    
        
    
          
    
            The curve says how much the whole plant gains today. That gain is shared out with an
            "ask, then tell" pair of walks over the plant. First every growing part is asked how
            much it wants (its demand), and the answers are added up. Then each part is told
            to take the same fraction of what it asked for: all of it if there's enough, less if
            there isn't. Anything left over is carried to the next day.
          
    
        
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
            A part's demand is how far it is from the size it's aiming for, but never more than it
            could grow in a day: an internode that needs at least 5 days to fill out asks for at
            most a fifth of its full weight each day.
          
    
        
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
          
    
            
    
            
    
            
    
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
            The numbers above come from running that day of the real plant with a probe on
            allocate(). Shoot growth (stems, leaves, buds) and reproductive growth
            (flowers, fruit) are shared out separately. Once flowering starts, a growing share of each
            day's gain goes to reproduction, until at maturity it reaches the plant's
            fraction of biomass in reproductive structures.
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
        
    
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    5. Meristems build phytomers
    
      
    
        
    
          
    
            A plant is built from repeated units botanists call phytomers: a length of stem
            (an internode), the leaf or pair of leaves at its top, and a bud in the angle of
            each leaf. The buds are meristems, the growing points. The meristem at the tip of a
            stem (the apical meristem) collects biomass, and once it has enough for a whole
            phytomer it builds one beneath itself and moves up to its top:
          
    
        
    
        
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
            So the data structure is a tree. Each internode has three slots: nextPlantPart
            (the stem carrying on), and leftBranchPlantPart and
            rightBranchPlantPart (the side buds, which may become branches or flower
            stalks). Below is that tree for a real plant, laid out flat so you can see the parts

      6. Branching and apical dominance
      
      
      
        
      
          
      
            Each day, every side bud that isn't growing yet considers becoming a branch. The chance
            is the plant's branching index, but in a real plant the growing tip releases a
            hormone that holds back buds close to it (apical dominance). PlantStudio models
            that with distance: a bud fewer than branching distance internodes below the tip
            gets a proportionally smaller chance.
          
      
        
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
          
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
            
            
      
          
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            A branch, once started, is just another stem with its own apical meristem, so it grows
            phytomers the same way. Whether branches can branch again is one more yes/no parameter.
          
      
        
      
      
      
    
      

    
      
      
      7. Flowers and fruit
      
      
      
        
      
          
      
            On the day the plant reaches its age at which flowering starts, every meristem
            gets a flowering signal, and each one becomes reproductive with a set probability. A
            reproductive meristem stops making stem and starts collecting biomass for an
            inflorescence, a flower stalk, which then makes its flowers a few at a time.
          
      
        
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            Each flower is a small state machine. It starts as a bud, opens once it has enough
            biomass and is old enough, and if it's a female flower that lives long enough it sets
            fruit, which ripens after a set number of days. Setting fruit throws away half the
            flower's weight, as the petals do.
          
      
        
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
          
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
          
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            One quirk kept on purpose: an inflorescence ages its flowers in its own nextDay(),
            and the day's walk then ages them again, so flowers age twice a day. The bundled plants
            were tuned with that behaviour, so changing it would change them.
          
      
        
      
      
      
    
      

    
      
      
      8. Drawing: the turtle walk
      
      
      
        
      
          
      
            Once the day's growth is done, the tree is drawn by walking it with a turtle: a
            pen with a position and a direction in 3D, kept as a 3×3 rotation matrix plus a point.
            Each internode turns the turtle a little and draws forward its current length; each
            branch saves the turtle's state with push() and gets it back with
            pop(), so the branch's turns don't affect the stem above it.
          
      
        
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            Angles are not degrees. A full turn is 256 steps, and sine and cosine come from a table
            of 256 entries made once at start-up, which was much faster than calling
            sin() on a 1990s PC. Rotations in degrees, like the plant's own turn and
            the angle between successive leaves, are converted with × 256 / 360; most
            angle parameters (branching, leaf and flower angles) go to the turtle as they are.
          
      
        
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            Two drawing parameters make a big difference. Between one phytomer and the next the
            turtle rolls around the stem by the phyllotactic angle. Most plants use about
            137.5°, the golden angle, which spreads leaves so none sits right above another. And each
            internode is drawn as a few shorter segments, sharing its bend between them, so stems
            curve instead of kinking:
          
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
          
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
            
          
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            The walk itself is a loop, not recursion, so a very long stem can't overflow the
            stack. Each internode remembers which of its three slots it's visiting next:
          
      
        
      
        
      
      
      
    
      

    
      
      
      9. Leaves and petals are 3D objects
      
      
      
        
      
          
      
            Stems are lines, but leaves, petals, sepals, fruit and buds are small triangle meshes
            (3D objects, .tdo), stored in the plant file as points and triangles.
            When a part draws one, every point is run through the turtle's current matrix and
            scaled by how grown the part is, so the same leaf shape appears at every node, turned
            and sized for where it is.
          
      
        
      
        
      
          
          
          
          
      
        
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            A flower is rings of these objects: pistils, stamens, up to five rows of petals, and
            sepals. Each ring turns the turtle around the flower's axis between copies, sharing out
            the 256 steps of a full turn so that a ring of any count closes evenly, then tips each
            copy back by its pull-back angle so it opens out.
          
      
        
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
          
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
            
            
      The picture is zoomed in on the plant's highest open flower.
      
          
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            Daisies and sunflowers are drawn differently. What looks like one flower is a
            head: an inflorescence that arranges many small flowers in a ring, each drawn
            with a single petal, the way the real plants are built.
          
      
        
      
      
      
    
      

    
      
      
      10. Pixels: sorting and rasterizing
      
      
      
        
      
          
      
            The turtle walk produces a list of lines and triangles in 3D, in the order the walk met
            them. Drawn in that order, a leaf behind the stem could be painted over it. So, like
            most 3D programs of its time, PlantStudio uses the painter's algorithm: sort
            everything by depth and paint from the back to the front, letting nearer things cover
            farther ones. Each 3D object is sorted as one piece, so a leaf never comes out
            interleaved with itself.
          
      
        
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
          
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
          
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            The pixels themselves come from a small software rasterizer instead of the browser's
            canvas drawing, because a canvas smooths every edge and the 1990s Windows look depends
            on hard ones. Lines use Bresenham's algorithm (with a disc stamped along them for wide
            stems, like Windows' round pens), and triangles are filled one horizontal span at a time:
          
      
        
      
        
      
      
      
    
      

    
      
      
      11. Breeding
      
      
      
        
      
          
      
            Since a plant is only numbers, breeding two plants is arithmetic. For each parameter the
            child takes a weighted average of its parents, then adds a random nudge drawn from a
            bell curve whose width is set by the mutation strength. Yes/no and choice parameters, 3D
            objects and curves come whole from one parent or the other.
          
      
        
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            
            ×
            
          
      
          
      
            
      
              
              
              
              
            
      
          
      
          
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            Colours are blended the way the original did it, as a single number holding red, green
            and blue, which is why bred plants sometimes come out in colours neither parent has.
          
      
        
      
      
      
    
      

    
      
      
      12. Past the curve: overgrowth
      
      
      
        
      
          
      
            This section isn't PlantStudio as written. It's a small switch added to this port's
            engine, off unless a page turns it on, which lets a plant keep growing after the
            original would have stopped it. With the switch off, every bundled plant draws exactly
            as it did before.
          
      
          
      
            A plant in PlantStudio stops growing in three places. The clock stops at the age of
            maturity. The S-curve can't go above 100% of the mature weight.
            And each part has its own limits: a stem or leaf only takes biomass for so many days,
            and only up to its own optimal weight. Overgrowth lets the clock run on and multiplies
            the plant's weight by the same amount every day past maturity, with no ceiling:
          
      
        
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            That alone does very little to most plants. Allocation only
            hands out what parts ask for. In most plants, by maturity every leaf and stem has
            stopped asking, and every waiting bud has been committed to a flower. So the new
            weight goes into a pile marked unallocated and stays there. To use it, the
            model needs somewhere to put it. Choose one of two places below.
          
      
          
      
            Old parts swell. Stems and leaves forget their day limits and aim for a multiple
            of their optimal weight, the multiple being how many times its mature weight the plant
            now has:
          
      
        
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            Dormant buds wake. While weight is left over, every side bud that's waiting
            starts growing, and a bud that was waiting to be a flower turns back into a shoot.
            Real plants do this too: it's called floral reversion, and it's where flowers
            with leafy shoots growing out of their middles come from.
          
      
        
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            
          
      
          
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
              Extra weight goes to
              
      
              
      
              
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
            
      
              
              
              
              
              
            
      
            
      
          
      
          
      
        
      
        
      
          
      
            Swelling makes a giant of the same plant: it keeps its shape and just gets bigger,
            because each part's length and width follow its weight. Waking buds makes an overgrown
            one: new shoots from every old flower bud, each making leaves and new buds of its own.
            A plant past its flowering age makes its new buds into flower buds, but while weight is
            left over those are woken into shoots too, the day after they appear. So the number of
            parts grows about as fast as the weight does, the old flowers turn to fruit, and no new
            ones ever open: the plant becomes all stem and leaf.
          
      
          
      
            The model has one more limit underneath all of these: the original never makes more
            than 10,000 parts in one plant. That cap still holds here, and at high growth rates
            the plant hits it, flattening the parts line on the chart while the weight carries on
            upward with nothing left to put it in.
